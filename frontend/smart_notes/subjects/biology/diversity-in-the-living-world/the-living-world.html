Diversity in the Living World

The Living World

Advanced Lecture 01 ⏱️ 25 min read 🎯 Exam Focus

1. Why study biological diversity? (Big picture)

At its core, biological diversity—or biodiversity—is the incredible variety of life on Earth. It isn't just about the number of different species, but about the variety at every single level, from the smallest differences in genes within a species, to the vast number of different species themselves, all the way up to the complex ecosystems they form, like rainforests and coral reefs.

Studying this diversity is fundamentally important for a few big reasons:

  • It's the Engine of Our Planet: Biodiversity is the foundation for how ecosystems work. Different species perform different jobs—like purifying water, pollinating crops, and cycling nutrients—that create a stable and resilient environment for all life, including us.
  • It's a Library of Solutions: This variety of life is a massive natural library of resources that humanity depends on. It provides us with everything from food and clean water to the chemical compounds that become life-saving medicines.
  • It's a History Book of Life: The patterns of diversity across the planet tell the story of Earth's history, showing us how life has evolved and adapted over millions of years.

For a student of biology, studying diversity is about connecting the dots. It helps to uncover the processes that create life's variety (like mutation, natural selection, and geological isolation) and to see the patterns that result from these processes (like why certain species are only found in certain places). The science of naming and classifying life, taxonomy, is the essential language that allows us to read this history book and understand both the patterns and the processes.

2. Levels of biological diversity

Biological diversity isn't just one thing; it's a concept that exists at three distinct and interconnected levels, from the microscopic to the global.

Figure 1: Visualizing the three interconnected levels of biodiversity.

Genetic Diversity (Variety Within a Species)

This is the diversity happening at the smallest scale. It's the sum of all the different genes (and their variations, called alleles) within a single species. Think of it as all the different colors, sizes, and disease resistances found in a population of dogs or tomato plants. This genetic variation is the raw material for adaptation and evolution, allowing species to survive in a changing world.

Species Diversity (Variety Among Species)

This is the level most people think of when they hear "biodiversity." It has two main components:

  • Richness: The total number of different species in a given area.
  • Evenness: The relative abundance of those species. An ecosystem with 20 different species that all have similar population sizes is considered more diverse than one where 95% of the individuals belong to just one species.

Ecosystem Diversity (Variety of Environments)

This is the diversity at the largest scale. It refers to the variety of different habitats, communities, and ecological processes on the planet. The difference between a desert, a wetland, a coral reef, and a mountain forest is a measure of ecosystem diversity.

🔗

The Key Connection

These levels are all linked. The processes that create variation at the genetic level (like mutation and recombination) are the ultimate source of the raw material that, over long evolutionary timescales, generates the diversity we see at the species and ecosystem levels. It all starts with the genes.

3. How to measure species diversity

The measurement of species diversity is a quantitative endeavor that involves several key metrics and indices. These tools allow for the objective comparison of biodiversity across different communities and habitats.

Fundamental Components of Diversity

  • Species Richness (S): The most fundamental measure of diversity, representing the total count of species present in a defined area or community. While simple to ascertain, it does not account for the relative abundances of those species.
  • Species Evenness: A measure of how equitably the individuals in a community are distributed among the different species. A community with high evenness has species with similar population sizes, whereas a community with low evenness is dominated by one or a few species.

Example: Richness vs. Evenness

Community Species A Species B Richness Evenness Diversity
Forest X 50 trees 50 trees 2 High Higher
Forest Y 99 trees 1 tree 2 Low Lower

Both forests have the same richness (2 species), but Forest X is more diverse because of higher evenness.

Scales of Diversity (Whittaker)

Ecologist R.H. Whittaker described diversity on three geographic scales:

α (Alpha) = Local Diversity

Diversity within a specific community or habitat (e.g., one field).

β (Beta) = Turnover

The change in species composition between two distinct communities (e.g., field vs. forest).

γ (Gamma) = Regional Diversity

Total diversity of a landscape (combines α and β).

The Species–Area Relationship

Figure 2: The Species-Area Relationship

The relationship between habitat area and species richness is a foundational concept in biogeography, mathematically described by the species–area relationship:

S = cAz
Where S = number of species, A = area of the habitat, and c & z are empirically derived constants.

Interpretation: The exponent z (the slope in a log-log plot) is a critical parameter. For isolated habitats such as islands, z values are typically steeper (ranging from 0.15 to 0.35), indicating that species richness is more sensitive to changes in area compared to contiguous continental habitats.

Common Diversity Indices

To combine richness and evenness into a single metric, ecologists use diversity indices.

1. Simpson's Index (D)

This index measures the probability that two individuals randomly selected from a sample will belong to the same species. A lower value of D indicates higher diversity.

D = ∑(pi)2
Where pi is the proportion of individuals belonging to species i.

Interpretation: The index is often reported as the Gini-Simpson Index (1 - D), where a higher value signifies greater diversity.

🧮

Example Calculation

For a community with species counts of {40, 30, 20, 10} (Total = 100):
Proportions (pi): {0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1}
D = (0.4)² + (0.3)² + (0.2)² + (0.1)² = 0.16 + 0.09 + 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.30.
Gini-Simpson Index (1 - D) = 0.70.

2. Shannon–Wiener Index (H')

Derived from information theory, this index quantifies the uncertainty in predicting the species identity of an individual chosen at random from the community. Higher values indicate greater uncertainty and thus higher diversity.

H' = -∑(pi*ln(pi))
Where pi is the proportion of individuals belonging to species i, and ln is the natural logarithm.

Interpretation: The Shannon–Wiener Index is particularly sensitive to the presence of rare species. It is a widely used metric for comparing the diversity of different ecological communities.

🧮

Example Calculation

For the same community proportions {0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1}:
H' = -[ (0.4 × ln0.4) + (0.3 × ln0.3) + (0.2 × ln0.2) + (0.1 × ln0.1) ]
H' = -[ (0.4 × -0.916) + (0.3 × -1.204) + (0.2 × -1.609) + (0.1 × -2.303) ]
H' = -[ -0.366 - 0.361 - 0.322 - 0.230 ] = -(-1.279) ≈ 1.28.

✨

Exam Focus

For examinations, it is crucial to know the formulas for these indices, be able to compute them for small datasets, and, most importantly, understand their ecological interpretation—specifically, how changes in species richness and evenness affect the final index value.

4. Taxonomy — Purpose and Core Principles

Taxonomy is the fundamental "filing system" for all of life. It's the science of identifying, naming, and classifying every living thing, from the smallest bacterium to the largest whale. Its purpose is to create a stable, organized, and informative framework that connects what an organism looks like (its phenotype), what its genes say (its genotype), and where it came from (its evolutionary history).

Figure 3: The hierarchical system of classification.

Taxonomy has three main jobs:

  • Recognize and Describe Species: The first job is to be the "librarian" of life, identifying and formally describing the basic units of biodiversity—the species.
  • Organize into a Family Tree: The second job is to arrange these species into a hierarchical system (like kingdom, phylum, class, etc.). This isn't just a random arrangement; it's designed to reflect the evolutionary "family tree" and show how different species are related to one another.
  • Give Everything a Stable Name: The third job is to provide a single, universal, and stable scientific name for every species. This nomenclature is crucial for scientists around the world to be able to communicate clearly without confusion.
💡

The Key Insight

It's important to understand that taxonomy is not just about giving things a label. It's a dynamic science of building hypotheses about relationships. When a taxonomist places two species in the same group, they are making a testable prediction that these two species share a common ancestor. As new information becomes available—especially powerful new DNA (molecular) data—these "family trees" are constantly being tested and revised, giving us a clearer and more accurate picture of the history of life.

Taxonomy vs. Systematics

While often used interchangeably, there is a subtle but important difference favored by examiners.

Feature Taxonomy Systematics
Focus Naming & Classification Evolutionary Relationships (Phylogeny)
Nature Static (Rules of Naming) Dynamic (Changes with new evolutionary data)
Scope Part of Systematics Broader (Taxonomy + Phylogeny)

5. Species — What is a species? (Multiple concepts)

The question "What is a species?" seems simple, but it is one of the most debated topics in biology. There is no single, universal definition that works perfectly for every organism on Earth. Instead, biologists use several different "species concepts," each with its own strengths and weaknesses.

Here are the most important ones:

1. The Biological Species Concept (BSC) - The "Reproduction" Rule

The Idea: This is the most famous definition. It says that a species is a group of individuals that can or could potentially interbreed in nature to produce fertile offspring, and they are reproductively isolated from other such groups.

  • Strength: It provides a clear, natural explanation for why different species (like dogs and cats) remain distinct.
  • Weaknesses: It completely fails for asexual organisms (like bacteria) that don't interbreed, for fossils (which can't reproduce), and for populations that are geographically separated (how can we know if they could interbreed if they never meet?).

2. The Morphological Species Concept - The "Looks" Rule

The Idea: This is the oldest and most practical concept. It defines a species based on a set of consistent physical differences. If it looks, acts, and sounds different from other groups, it's a different species.

  • Strength: It's incredibly useful for field identification and is the only concept that works for fossils.
  • Weaknesses: It can be misleading. Convergent evolution can make two unrelated species look very similar. Conversely, cryptic species are two or more distinct species that look identical but are genetically different and cannot interbreed.

3. The Phylogenetic Species Concept - The "Family Tree" Rule

The Idea: This modern, genetics-based concept defines a species as the smallest distinct branch on the evolutionary tree. It's a group that shares a unique, common ancestor and has a set of shared, derived characteristics that no other group has.

  • Strength: It's a powerful concept because it is based on evolutionary history and works for all organisms, including asexual ones and fossils.
  • Weakness: Its application can sometimes lead to what some biologists call "oversplitting," where tiny, genetically distinct but otherwise similar populations are each declared a separate species.

4. The Ecological Species Concept - The "Job" Rule

The Idea: This concept defines a species by its unique ecological role or "job" (niche). A group of organisms that uses environmental resources in a distinct way from all other groups is considered a separate species.

✨

Exam Tip

Be prepared to compare and contrast these different concepts. It's important to know not just what they are, but also to be able to give an example of an organism or situation where one concept works well and another one fails.

6. Mechanisms Driving Origin of Species (Speciation)

Speciation is the evolutionary process by which new biological species arise. For this to happen, two key ingredients are absolutely essential: there must be existing genetic variation within a population, and a barrier to reproduction (reproductive isolation) must emerge that stops gene flow.

The process of speciation is generally categorized into a few main "modes," based on the geography of the diverging populations.

The Main Modes of Speciation

  • Allopatric Speciation (The "Different Homeland" Mode): This is the most common and intuitive mode. It happens when a population is split into two or more groups by a geographic barrier—like a new mountain range, a river, or a glacier. Once separated, the isolated populations begin to evolve independently due to genetic drift and natural selection, eventually becoming so different that they can no longer interbreed.
  • Peripatric Speciation (The "Founder" Mode): A special case of allopatric speciation. It occurs when a very small group breaks off from the main population and colonizes a new, isolated habitat. This small "founder" group can diverge very rapidly due to strong genetic drift (the founder effect) and new selective pressures.
  • Parapatric Speciation (The "Neighbor" Mode): This mode occurs when two populations are not completely separated but live right next to each other across a sharp environmental gradient (like the boundary between a polluted and a clean area). Even though there is some limited gene flow between them, strong selective pressures cause the neighboring populations to diverge.
  • Sympatric Speciation (The "Same Homeland" Mode): The most controversial but fascinating mode. It happens when a new species evolves from a single ancestral species while inhabiting the same geographic region. This can occur through mechanisms like a "host shift" (where a group of insects begins to feed on a new type of plant) or through polyploidy (a type of mutation involving whole sets of chromosomes, which is very common in plants).

The Barriers to Reproduction (Isolating Mechanisms)

These are the "walls" that prevent two diverging groups from mixing their genes again. They come in two main types:

1. Pre-zygotic Barriers (Before Fertilization)

These barriers act before a zygote (fertilized egg) can be formed.

  • Habitat Isolation: Live in different places.
  • Temporal Isolation: Breed at different times (day, season, year).
  • Behavioral Isolation: Different courtship rituals or calls.
  • Mechanical Isolation: Reproductive parts don't fit.
  • Gametic Isolation: Sperm and egg are incompatible.

2. Post-zygotic Barriers (After Fertilization)

These barriers act after a zygote is formed, making offspring unviable or sterile.

  • Hybrid Inviability: Dies before ends maturity.
  • Hybrid Sterility: Survives but is sterile (e.g., Mule).
  • Hybrid Breakdown: First gen fertile, next gen weak/sterile.
⚡

Conceptual Strategy for Problems

When faced with a speciation problem, the key is to think like an evolutionary biologist. Ask three questions:

  1. Is there still gene flow, or are the populations geographically separated?
  2. What specific barriers to reproduction could be developing?
  3. What is different about their environments that could be causing them to diverge through natural selection or genetic drift?

8. Binomial Nomenclature — The Rules of Naming

Who started this?

Carolus Linnaeus is the father of taxonomy and the system of Binomial Nomenclature.

The 4 Universal Rules

  • Latin Origin: Biological names are generally in Latin and written in italics. They are Latinised or derived from Latin irrespective of their origin.
  • Two Components: The first word representing the genus is called the Generic name. The second word identifying the species is called the Specific epithet.
  • Formatting (Handwritten vs. Typed):
    • When handwritten: Both words are underlined separately.
    • When printed: Both words are in italics.
  • Capitalization:
    • Genus: Starts with a Capital letter.
    • Species: Starts with a small letter.
⚠️

Common Trap

Examiners love to swap the capitalization.
Correct: Mangifera indica
Incorrect: Mangifera Indica (Capital 'I')
Incorrect: mangifera indica (Small 'm')

9. The 3-Domain System (Carl Woese)

Carl Woese (1990) proposed this system based on rRNA gene sequences. It replaces the older 5-Kingdom classification by splitting the Kingdom Monera into two domains.

The Three Domains

Figure 4: Phylogenetic Tree of the Three Domains

1. Domain Archaea

Ancient, primitive bacteria (prokaryotes) that often live in extreme environments (extremophiles like halophiles, thermophiles). Unrelated to "true" bacteria.

2. Domain Bacteria

True bacteria (prokaryotes). Includes pathogens, cyanobacteria, and beneficial gut flora. Cell walls contain peptidoglycan.

3. Domain Eukarya

All organisms with eukaryotic cells (nucleus + organelles). Includes Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia.

✨

IISER Exam Insight

Examiners prefer the 3-Domain System over the traditional 5-Kingdom system because it more accurately reflects evolutionary history (phylogeny). Remember: Archaea are evolutionarily closer to Eukarya than to Bacteria!

Reading Phylogenies

Cladogram vs Phylogram: Cladograms show branching order only. Phylograms add branch lengths proportional to time.

Key Terms

  • Monophyletic (Clade): Ancestor + ALL descendants.
  • Paraphyletic: Ancestor + SOME descendants. (Avoid)
  • Synapomorphy: Shared derived character.
